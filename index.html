How labeling competitive norms as “White culture” shapes expectation and mobility

01 What is competition?

Competition is how people pursue limited rewards — grades, jobs, status — under shared rules. It’s a social tool, not inherently good or evil. Yet recent educational materials frame the habits that support it (urgency, individualism, objectivity, time-keeping) as “white culture” or “white supremacy culture.”

Duke’s plan treated work-style expectations the same way. Pattern: competitive norms are racialized and treated as harmful.

Conflict theory: Students with fewer resources are less likely to gain mobility skills — inequality gets reproduced.

Haidt — Safetyism ▶

Treating emotional comfort as sacred makes students more fragile. Humans need measured challenge. Protecting them from competition and ranking removes the experiences that build resilience under pressure.

Sowell — Cosmic justice ▶

Ordinary justice = same rules for everyone. Cosmic justice = adjusting standards to equalize outcomes. Lowering standards or shielding groups from competition weakens effort and long-term skill development.

Competition is a tool. How we label it changes what it produces. Racializing competitive traits elevates race to a master status and can weaken both expectations and incentives. If the goal is higher achievement — especially for students who are behind — structured competition and clear standards remain the strongest tools available.

I examined materials I’d been suspicious of while watching my younger siblings struggle in public school. I want schools to be fair and open to every student. But racializing competitiveness, “safetyism,” and the quest for “cosmic justice” are regressive practices that harm the students already struggling. The NAEP data supports that conclusion.
