THE CASE FOR
COMPETITION

A sociological look at why competition matters, how it got reframed, and what the numbers actually show.

01 What is competition?

Competition is a rules based way people and groups go after limited rewards - grades, resources, status, jobs. It’s not automatically good or bad. It’s a form of social technology.

Why sociologists care about it ▶

It shapes motivation (structural functionalism), sorts cultural capital, builds institutional hierarchies (tracking, rankings, accountability), and has become something people debate over and label.

When the rules and social norms around competition change, the outcomes change too.

Key sociological concepts in play ▶
  • Labeling theory + master status — when race becomes the main lens for judging normal behavior
  • Pygmalion effect — expectations change performance
  • Cultural capital — which skills and habits get rewarded
  • Social construction of race — race meanings are made, not found
  • Institutional processes — how ideas move from theories into policy and practice

02 How competition got labeled

In a bunch of recent institutional materials, traits that usually support competition got rebranded as racial or harmful.

Real examples (click to expand) ▶
  • Tema Okun's “White Supremacy Culture” essay (1999–2001): lists sense of urgency, perfectionism, individualism, objectivity, etc. as features of white supremacy culture. It continues to appear in teacher professional development, school district equity plans, university training modules, and resources distributed by the National Education Association (NEA).
  • Smithsonian "White Culture" graphic (2020): listed politeness, hard work, punctuality, delayed gratification, and “objective, rational linear thinking” as aspects of White culture. It was later taken down.
  • Duke Medical School plan (2021): treated expectations of timeliness, dress, speech, and work style as part of “white supremacy culture.”
  • Washington State equity official, Dr. Karen Johnson (2020): contrasted “white people and Europeans” focus on agendas and time with how “people of color” approach time; public statement about “decolonizing” time requirements.
  • An Equity & Excellence in Education article called “Stalling White Time” (2025): argues that expectations of fast pacing, controlled efficiency, and “sense of urgency” in classroom management constitute a racialized “white time”.
  • Ongoing institutional adoption in K–12 and higher education: Scholarly literature in education routinely calls for “dismantling white supremacy culture” and treating curriculum, standards, and institutional norms as property of whiteness that must be "de-centered".

Same pattern: everyday norms or dominant group assumptions get racialized.

What the mechanisms do ▶

Once competitive norms get labeled as racially suspect, the label starts organizing how people see effort, ranking, and high standards. Race becomes a master status (social category that overrides all others in shaping how a person is perceived and treated). Educator and student expectations shift (Pygmalion). Skills that help in competitive settings their recognized value as cultural capital. Institutions then change grading, accountability, and classroom talk.

03 Psychology & incentives

Jonathan Haidt & “The Coddling of the American Mind” ▶

Haidt and co-author Lukianoff argue that “safetyism” - treating emotional comfort as sacred - makes young people more fragile, not stronger. They theorize that humans are antifragile: we need some challenge and stress (in reasonable doses) to grow.

When schools protect students from competition, criticism, and ranking, they remove the exact experiences that build resilience. Anxiety goes up, tolerance for discomfort goes down, and performance under pressure suffers.

Thomas Sowell on incentives & “cosmic justice” ▶

Sowell distinguishes regular justice (same rules for everyone) from “cosmic justice” (trying to equalize outcomes by changing standards or shielding groups from competition).

He warns that when you lower standards or protect people from competitive pressure - even with good intentions - you often weaken incentives and produce worse long-term skill development. Softening competition doesn’t erase disparities; it can lock them in.

04 The actual numbers

Here’s what the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) shows.

Approximate trajectory from published NAEP LTT results. Reading 2023 ≈ 256 (near 1971). Math fell sharply after 2012.

More precise data points ▶
  • Age 9 (2022): Reading still +7 vs 1971 but –5 from 2020. Math +15 vs 1973 but –7 from 2020.
  • Age 13 (2023): Reading 4 pts lower than 2020 and 7 pts lower than a decade earlier. Math 9 pts lower than 2020 and 14 pts lower than 2012.
  • Racial gaps narrowed a lot from the 1970s into the early 2000s as Black and Hispanic students gained faster. Progress later stalled and in some cases reversed amid the overall declines.

05 Putting it together

Two big shifts line up with the stagnation and drop:

  • The high-accountability No Child Left Behind Act era (strong federal competitive pressure on public schools) overlapped with rising or stable scores and gap-narrowing.
  • After Every Student Succeeds Act (2015) reduced pressure, and as cultural critiques of competition, high standards, and anti-meritocracy language spread, scores fell - especially for lower-performing students.

Haidt’s safetyism + Sowell’s "cosmic justice" warning + the labeling of competitive norms all point the same direction: when you remove structured challenge and make high effort culturally awkward, the institutional rewards for getting better get weaker.

This is correlation, not a claim of single-cause effect. Phones, social media, family changes, teaching methods, and COVID all matter too. But the sociological mechanisms are real and measurable.

06 Functionalism & Conflict Theory

Two classic sociological lenses help explain why the framing of competition matters so much.

Structural Functionalism view ▶

From a functionalist perspective, competition is one of the mechanisms that keeps complex societies running. It helps:

  • Sort people into roles based on performance and effort
  • Motivate individuals to develop skills
  • Allocate limited rewards (grades, jobs, status) in a relatively open way
  • Maintain standards that institutions rely on to function properly

When competitive norms are weakened or morally stigmatized, functionalists would expect downstream problems: lower collective skill levels and reduced system efficiency. The NAEP trends are consistent with that prediction.

Conflict Theory view ▶

Conflict theory sees schools and cultural messages as arenas of power. Teaching working poor and underclass children that competition is harmful, unfair, or racially suspect can function as a form of social control.

If students from lower socioeconomic positions internalize the idea that striving, ranking, and high standards are illegitimate, they are less likely to develop the competitive skills and cultural capital needed to move into higher-status/higher class positions. The result is reduced social mobility and the reproduction of existing class inequalities - even when the message is delivered with anti-oppression language.

In short: discouraging competition among those who start with fewer resources can lock them into the lower class rather than giving them tools to rise.

How this ties back to the rest of the analysis ▶

Labeling competitive traits as racially or morally problematic (Okun, institutional materials, public statements) changes the expectancy climate (Pygmalion) and devalues performance-oriented cultural capital. Functionalism predicts system-level skill loss. Conflict theory predicts that the skill loss will fall hardest on those who already have less, reinforcing class boundaries.

Both lenses point to the same practical conclusion: removing structured competition does not automatically produce equity. It often produces lower performance and reduced social mobility.

07 Takeaways

The short version ▶
  • Competition is a tool. How we label and institutionalize it changes what it produces.
  • Racializing normal competitive traits (urgency, standards, effort) turns race into a master status and can lower expectations.
  • Protecting people from challenge (Haidt) and removing competitive incentives (Sowell) both tend to weaken skill development.
  • National literacy and test data show earlier gains, then stagnation and decline that line up with the softening of competitive pressure and the cultural reframing of high standards.
  • If the goal is higher actual skill - especially for students who are currently behind - structured competition and clear standards remain some of the strongest tools we have.
